Laredo is noticeably drier than the Lower Rio Grande Valley (RGV) overall, though the exact contrast depends on where in the lower Valley you compare it to.  
The primary driver is the **west-to-east rainfall gradient** across South Texas. As you move east toward the Gulf Coast, Gulf moisture increases average rainfall and humidity, while moving inland toward Laredo shifts the climate into semi-arid brushland.

| Feature | Laredo | Lower RGV (McAllen / Edinburg) | Lower RGV (Brownsville / Harlingen) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Annual Rainfall** | \~20–21 inches | \~22–23 inches | \~27–28 inches |
| **Climate Classification** | Hot Semi-Arid (*BSh*) | Semi-Arid to Subhumid | Deep South Subtropical / Humid Subhumid |
| **Humidity & Dew Point** | Lower humidity overall; lower night/winter dew points | Moderate Gulf humidity | High coastal humidity & persistent dew points |
| **Summer Heat Feel** | Dry, intense heat; more frequent 100°F+ days | High heat \+ moderate humidity | High heat index due to intense Gulf moisture |

### **Key Differences**

* **Rainfall Volatility:** While 20 inches in Laredo vs. 22 inches in McAllen doesn't look like a huge difference on paper, Laredo experiences higher evapotranspiration rates and longer dry spells. Rain in Laredo comes largely in short bursts during late spring and early autumn thunderstorm spikes.  
* **Coastal Moisture vs. Continental Heat:** Lower RGV cities close to the Gulf coast (like Brownsville) receive nearly **7 to 8 more inches of rain per year** than Laredo. Even when the Lower RGV is not actively raining, the persistent muggy air mass off the Gulf keeps soil and vegetation far more hydrated than the sun-baked inland brush of Webb County.  
* **Winter Driest Period:** Winter in Laredo is notoriously dry (averaging under 1 inch per month from November through February). The lower coast maintains slightly higher cool-season moisture.
